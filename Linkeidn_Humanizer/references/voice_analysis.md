## 1. What the human-sounding posts do

- **Short and single-minded.** Most are 10 to 90 words and carry one thought. Examples: a one-line thank-you, a one-line tease, a short reaction.
- **A person is visibly present.** First-person lines like "Saw Alex ask Gemini... So I asked...", "I'm one of a few hundred people on the planet wearing one", "I had a ton of questions lined up".
- **Reaction openers.** "Guys,", "Crazy how...", "It was super cool to...", "Yes, it's a...". They sound like speech, not headlines.
- **Loose, spoken punctuation.** Run-ons, missing commas or periods where a thought is joined, an occasional lowercase mid-sentence, informal spellings ("offcourse", "cant"), "Haha :)".
- **Casual asides and jokes.** "Btw", "Let me explain 👇", self-aware humour ("ask it to write a birthday wish 😅").
- **Emoji as a reaction.** Usually one, in the hook or the last line (🤯 🔥 🙏 👀 🤣 ❤️). Not scattered as decoration.
- **Attitude and opinion.** "Come back in ten years and tell me which approach produced better engineers." "We don't get to opt out."
- **Plain endings.** A direct question to the reader ("Would you trust JioPC with real work?"), a tease ("I wish I could say more 👀"), a thank-you, or no ending at all.
- **Lived detail.** Real places, names, moments, and stakes ("middle-class kid from India", "2 AM, no cofounder to call").

## 2. What the AI-sounding posts do

- **Neutral wire-service voice.** Third-person summary of a news item or a Reddit story, with no person in it. Pronouns sometimes drift because a summary was machine-made.
- **The antithesis move.** "It reads like a boring company update. It is actually a countdown." "That's not attention. That's intent." "Your slow TV does not need replacing. It needs debloating."
- **Tidy chiasmus and aphorism closers.** "The spec is where the thinking happens. The code is where the thinking gets typed out." "One giant mind has no checks. A trillion smaller ones check each other."
- **Stock transition phrases.** "The catch is the obvious one." "But here is the catch nobody is talking about." "Think about that first one." "That list is the real story." "The scary part:" "This one is big." "Some things that stood out:" "The lesson is simple."
- **Fact dumps and digests.** Dense numbers, nested "→" lists, weekly recaps, org-chart style detail, press-release phrasing.
- **Even rhythm.** Similar sentence lengths, perfect grammar, no fragments that feel spoken.
- **Fragment runs for drama.** "Navigation. Hazards. Delivery tasks." "Calls him... Calls itself... Demands..." "For now."
- **Engagement-bait closers.** "Thoughts?", "Which story should we go deeper on?", a question added only to farm comments.
- **Sponsored or partner copy.** Corporate phrasing, hashtag blocks, generic theses ("commoditized", "across the entire customer lifecycle").

## 3. What transfers to a different author and topic

Copy the mechanics, not the content:
1. Put a real person in the post: what they saw, tried, felt, or got wrong.
2. Keep it to one thought and cut the rest.
3. Open like speech, with one concrete anchor.
4. Vary rhythm: fragments, medium sentences, one run-on.
5. Say an opinion plainly, with a little attitude.
6. End plainly: a real question, a blunt line, thanks, or stop.
7. Use at most one reaction emoji and one casual aside.
8. Specific beats general: a number, a name, a place, a moment.

## 4. What must never be copied

- Topics, sentences, phrases, or signature lines from the reference posts.
- The AI-bucket patterns above, even though some also appear in the "hybrid" human-bucket posts (for example the polished contrast lines in the Meta Connect post). From hybrid posts, take only the human parts.
- The recurring WhatsApp-community call-to-action. It is a template, not a voice signal.
- Fabricated experiences. The reference authors had real events to write about. The agent must only use events the user provides.
